# Core principles from mature digital card games

Lessons distilled from Pokémon TCG, Magic: The Gathering, Hearthstone, Legends of Runeterra, Marvel Snap,
Slay the Spire, Monster Train and Inscryption. These describe *why* the games work — never copy their
cards, numbers or rules.

## 1. A card is a contextual decision, not a button
A card's value must change with the situation: timing, target, what else is in hand, what the enemy is
about to do. Pokémon's "which Pokémon gets this turn's one Energy attachment" is a tiny decision repeated
every turn that shapes the whole game. If a card is always correct to play immediately on the obvious
target, it adds no decision.

## 2. Scarcity is the engine of choice
Players must have more good options than they can afford. Slay the Spire gives ~5 cards and 3 Energy;
Hearthstone gives a hand of options against a mana curve. The tension "I can do two of these three things"
is the turn. If resources cover everything, or cover exactly one fixed set of actions, there is no choice.

## 3. Drawing is new information plus new options
Anticipation comes from **uncertainty that matters**: the next card could change the plan. That needs:
a deck you can reason about (you know what *could* come), enough variance that you don't know what
*will* come, and cards whose arrival changes what you do this turn. Marvel Snap's small deck with one
draw per turn makes every draw visible and consequential.

## 4. Card advantage and tempo are the real currencies
(Magic.) Cards are resources; so is time. A play can trade cards for tempo (act now) or tempo for cards
(set up later). Good designs make players weigh "use it now" against "hold it for the right moment".

## 5. Telegraphs turn threat into planning
Slay the Spire's enemy intents, Runeterra's attack token and Snap's location reveals let players plan a
response. A visible threat creates the defensive decision; a hidden one only creates frustration.

## 6. Setup → payoff, with a felt power spike
Pokémon evolution, Snap's turn-6 swing, a deckbuilder combo coming online. Players accept quieter setup
turns when a payoff is visible and coming. The payoff must look and feel dramatically bigger.

## 7. Synergy beats raw stats
Cards gain value from interactions — with a character, a tribe, another card, a board state. Runeterra
champions level up by doing their thing; Hearthstone tribes reward building around them. Synergy creates
"this card is great *here*" moments and makes the same card play differently in different hands.

## 8. Every turn must visibly change the board
Pokémon attacks take a big, readable chunk of HP; most KOs take 2–4 attacks. If a turn's actions barely
move a bar, the player stops believing their choices matter. Damage-to-HP ratio is a feel decision, not
just a balance one.

## 9. Limit *kinds* of actions, not only the amount
Pokémon: one Energy attachment, one Supporter, one attack per turn. Magic: one land per turn. Per-type
limits create texture ("which Supporter this turn?") without adding currencies.

## 10. Readability before depth
The player must predict cost, effect, target and outcome *before* committing, then see the result
explained (animation, numbers, log). If a result surprises the player in a way they can't trace, agency
is lost.

## 11. Randomness belongs on the input side
Random *draws* (before you decide) create decisions. Random *outcomes after* you decide (miss chance,
damage rolls) can erase decisions. Prefer input randomness; keep output randomness rare and readable.

## 12. Complexity must pay rent
Every rule costs player attention. Add a rule only if it creates a decision the loop needs. Prove the
small loop is fun before layering systems on it.
